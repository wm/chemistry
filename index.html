Charges, electrons, and static electricity

Everything in this guide comes down to one idea: in everyday charging, electrons move and protons stay put. Start by changing the electrons on a lithium atom.

Li
3protons (+)
3electrons (−)
4neutrons (0)
0net charge
Neutral lithium atom

Net charge = protons − electrons. Notice the proton count never changes. That's what keeps it lithium.

Reading an ion's symbol

The superscript on a symbol tells you the net charge, and the net charge tells you how electrons compare with protons. It does not tell you anything about neutrons. Those depend on the isotope (most lithium has 4).

SymbolProtonsElectronsNet chargeWhat happened
Li330Neutral atom. Positive and negative charges balance exactly.
Li⁺32+1Lost one electron. Fewer negatives than positives, so it is a positive ion (cation).
Li⁻34−1Gained one electron. More negatives than positives, so it is a negative ion (anion).

Why is Li⁺ common and Li⁻ rare? Lithium's third electron sits alone in the outer shell, far from the nucleus, with two inner electrons partly blocking the nucleus's pull. It is easy to remove. Li⁻ can exist under special conditions, but in nature and in the lab lithium almost always shows up as Li⁺.

The pattern to remember: a positive charge means missing electrons, not extra protons. A negative charge means extra electrons.

What can move, and what can't

ParticleChargeWhere it isCan it move from one object to another by rubbing?
Proton+1Locked in the nucleusNo. Protons are held in the nucleus by the strong nuclear force. Moving one would change which element the atom is.
Neutron0Locked in the nucleusNo, and it has no charge anyway, so it plays no part in static electricity.
Electron−1Outside the nucleus, with outer electrons farthest outYes. Outer electrons are the least tightly held particles, and they are on the surface where objects touch.

Charge is never created or destroyed. When one object becomes positive, another object becomes equally negative, because the electrons just changed places. This is called conservation of charge.

The rules of attraction

Charged particles push or pull on each other without touching. That push or pull is the electric force, and it follows three rules:

Opposite charges attract. Positive pulls on negative.
Like charges repel. Negative pushes negative, positive pushes positive.
Closer means stronger. Halve the distance and the force becomes about four times stronger.

Electron transfer: balloon and hair

Both objects start neutral, each with equal numbers of positive and negative charges. Rubbing brings the surfaces into close contact, and electrons move from the hair to the balloon because the balloon's rubber holds electrons more strongly than hair does. Things to notice in the model: the + signs never move. Only − signs cross from hair to balloon. The hair's charge and the balloon's charge are always equal in size and opposite in sign. And the strands of hair, now all positive, spread apart from each other as well as reaching toward the balloon.

Why a charged object can attract a neutral one

A charged balloon will also stick to a wall, even though the wall has no net charge. No electrons jump between them. Instead, the balloon's extra electrons repel the electrons in the wall's atoms, pushing them slightly to the far side of each atom. That leaves the side of each atom nearest the balloon slightly positive.

This is called polarization. The nearby positive side is closer to the balloon than the shifted negative side, and since closer means stronger, the attraction wins over the repulsion.

Same atom, different models

No single model shows everything. Each one makes some features easy to see by leaving others out. Scientists switch between models depending on the question.

A useful habit: when you explain something, say which model you are using and why it fits. The shell model is good for counting electrons and seeing why lithium loses one. The plus-and-minus model is good for tracking which object gained or lost charge. The cloud model is good for explaining polarization.

Two scales, one event

Static electricity happens at two scales at once. At the macroscopic scale you see objects move. At the atomic scale, too small to see, electrons move. A complete explanation connects the two: what you saw, and the particle-level cause of it.

Macroscopic scale (what you see)Atomic scale (what's happening)
You rub two objects together.Surface atoms come into close contact, and electrons transfer from one material to the other.
Nothing looks different about the objects.One object now has extra electrons, and the other is missing the same number. Each has a small net charge.
The objects pull toward each other.Opposite net charges attract through the electric force.
Strands of hair stand up and spread apart.Every strand lost electrons, so every strand is positive, and like charges repel.
You feel a small shock touching a doorknob.Extra electrons rush between you and the metal all at once to even out the charge imbalance.
The attraction fades after a while.Electrons slowly leak to or from the air (especially humid air) until both objects are neutral again.

Key words

ElectronNegative particle outside the nucleus. The only particle that moves between objects in static electricity.
ProtonPositive particle in the nucleus. Its number defines the element and does not change.
ChargeA property of particles that causes electric force. Objects get a net charge when electrons are gained or lost.
PositiveFewer electrons than protons. The object lost electrons.
NegativeMore electrons than protons. The object gained electrons.
NeutralEqual numbers of electrons and protons, so no net charge.
AttractPull together. Happens between opposite charges.
RepelPush apart. Happens between like charges.
IonAn atom with a net charge because it gained or lost electrons, like Li⁺.
PolarizationElectrons in a neutral object shifting to one side because a charged object is nearby.

Check yourself

Try answering each one out loud before opening it.

An oxygen atom has 8 protons. O²⁻ has how many electrons?

10. A 2− charge means two more electrons than protons: 8 + 2 = 10.

A sock comes out of the dryer with a positive charge. Did it gain protons?

No. Protons are locked in nuclei. The sock lost electrons to something else in the dryer, which is now negative by the same amount.

A wool cloth gives 1,000 electrons to a plastic rod. What is the charge on each?

The rod is −1,000 (in electron charges) and the cloth is +1,000. Total charge before and after is zero.

Two balloons are both rubbed on the same sweater. What happens when you hold them near each other?

They repel. Both took electrons from the sweater, so both are negative, and like charges repel.

Why does a charged object attract tiny bits of neutral paper?

The charged object polarizes the paper's atoms, shifting their electrons. The side of the paper nearest the object ends up with the opposite charge, and because it is closer, the attraction is stronger than the repulsion.

Which model would you pick to show why lithium tends to form Li⁺ instead of Li⁻?

The shell (Bohr) model, because it shows the single electron alone in the outer shell, far from the nucleus and easy to remove.

When you write an explanation, aim for three parts: what you observed, what happened to the electrons, and which rule of charge explains the result.
