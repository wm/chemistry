Charges, electrons, and static electricity

Everything in this guide comes down to one idea: in everyday charging, electrons move and protons stay put. Start by changing the electrons on a lithium atom.

Li
3protons (+)
3electrons (−)
4neutrons (0)
0net charge
Neutral lithium atom

Net charge = protons − electrons. Notice the proton count never changes. That's what keeps it lithium.

Reading an ion's symbol

The superscript on a symbol tells you the net charge, and the net charge tells you how electrons compare with protons. It does not tell you anything about neutrons. Those depend on the isotope (most lithium has 4).

SymbolProtonsElectronsNet chargeWhat happened
Li330Neutral atom. Positive and negative charges balance exactly.
Li⁺32+1Lost one electron. Fewer negatives than positives, so it is a positive ion (cation).
Li⁻34−1Gained one electron. More negatives than positives, so it is a negative ion (anion).

Why is Li⁺ common and Li⁻ rare? Lithium's third electron sits alone in the outer shell, far from the nucleus, with two inner electrons partly blocking the nucleus's pull. It is easy to remove. Li⁻ can exist under special conditions, but in nature and in the lab lithium almost always shows up as Li⁺.

The pattern to remember: a positive charge means missing electrons, not extra protons. A negative charge means extra electrons.

What can move, and what can't

ParticleChargeWhere it isCan it move from one object to another by rubbing?
Proton+1Locked in the nucleusNo. Protons are held in the nucleus by the strong nuclear force. Moving one would change which element the atom is.
Neutron0Locked in the nucleusNo, and it has no charge anyway, so it plays no part in static electricity.
Electron−1Outside the nucleus, with outer electrons farthest outYes. Outer electrons are the least tightly held particles, and they are on the surface where objects touch.

Charge is never created or destroyed. When one object becomes positive, another object becomes equally negative, because the electrons just changed places. This is called conservation of charge.

The rules of attraction

Charged particles push or pull on each other without touching. That push or pull is the electric force, and it follows three rules:

Opposite charges attract. Positive pulls on negative.
Like charges repel. Negative pushes negative, positive pushes positive.
Closer means stronger. Halve the distance and the force becomes about four times stronger.

Flip the signs and move the slider to test each rule.

Electron transfer: balloon and hair

Both objects start neutral, each with equal numbers of positive and negative charges. Rubbing brings the surfaces into close contact, and electrons move from the hair to the balloon because the balloon's rubber holds electrons more strongly than hair does. Rub a few times, then look at what the hair does.

0electrons moved
0hair charge
0balloon charge

Things to notice in the model: the + signs never move. Only − signs cross from hair to balloon. The hair's charge and the balloon's charge are always equal in size and opposite in sign. And the strands of hair, now all positive, spread apart from each other as well as reaching toward the balloon.

Why a charged object can attract a neutral one

A charged balloon will also stick to a wall, even though the wall has no net charge. No electrons jump between them. Instead, the balloon's extra electrons repel the electrons in the wall's atoms, pushing them slightly to the far side of each atom. That leaves the side of each atom nearest the balloon slightly positive.

This is called polarization. The nearby positive side is closer to the balloon than the shifted negative side, and since closer means stronger, the attraction wins over the repulsion.

Same atom, different models

No single model shows everything. Each one makes some features easy to see by leaving others out. Scientists switch between models depending on the question.

A useful habit: when you explain something, say which model you are using and why it fits. The shell model is good for counting electrons and seeing why lithium loses one. The plus-and-minus model is good for tracking which object gained or lost charge. The cloud model is good for explaining polarization.

Two scales, one event

Static electricity happens at two scales at once. At the macroscopic scale you see objects move. At the atomic scale, too small to see, electrons move. A complete explanation connects the two: what you saw, and the particle-level cause of it.

Macroscopic scale (what you see)Atomic scale (what's happening)
You rub two objects together.Surface atoms come into close contact, and electrons transfer from one material to the other.
Nothing looks different about the objects.One object now has extra electrons, and the other is missing the same number. Each has a small net charge.
The objects pull toward each other.Opposite net charges attract through the electric force.
Strands of hair stand up and spread apart.Every strand lost electrons, so every strand is positive, and like charges repel.
You feel a small shock touching a doorknob.Extra electrons rush between you and the metal all at once to even out the charge imbalance.
The attraction fades after a while.Electrons slowly leak to or from the air (especially humid air) until both objects are neutral again.

Key words

ElectronNegative particle outside the nucleus. The only particle that moves between objects in static electricity.
ProtonPositive particle in the nucleus. Its number defines the element and does not change.
ChargeA property of particles that causes electric force. Objects get a net charge when electrons are gained or lost.
PositiveFewer electrons than protons. The object lost electrons.
NegativeMore electrons than protons. The object gained electrons.
NeutralEqual numbers of electrons and protons, so no net charge.
AttractPull together. Happens between opposite charges.
RepelPush apart. Happens between like charges.
IonAn atom with a net charge because it gained or lost electrons, like Li⁺.
PolarizationElectrons in a neutral object shifting to one side because a charged object is nearby.

Check yourself

Try answering each one out loud before opening it.

An oxygen atom has 8 protons. O²⁻ has how many electrons?

10. A 2− charge means two more electrons than protons: 8 + 2 = 10.

A sock comes out of the dryer with a positive charge. Did it gain protons?

No. Protons are locked in nuclei. The sock lost electrons to something else in the dryer, which is now negative by the same amount.

A wool cloth gives 1,000 electrons to a plastic rod. What is the charge on each?

The rod is −1,000 (in electron charges) and the cloth is +1,000. Total charge before and after is zero.

Two balloons are both rubbed on the same sweater. What happens when you hold them near each other?

They repel. Both took electrons from the sweater, so both are negative, and like charges repel.

Why does a charged object attract tiny bits of neutral paper?

The charged object polarizes the paper's atoms, shifting their electrons. The side of the paper nearest the object ends up with the opposite charge, and because it is closer, the attraction is stronger than the repulsion.

Which model would you pick to show why lithium tends to form Li⁺ instead of Li⁻?

The shell (Bohr) model, because it shows the single electron alone in the outer shell, far from the nucleus and easy to remove.

The checklist

protons = atomic number (decides the element) mass = protons + neutrons charge = protons − electrons
  1. Particle table? Find the row's two known numbers and use the rule that connects them. Each row needs at most two of the three rules.
  2. “Neutral” means protons = electrons. A + charge means missing electrons; a − charge means extra electrons. Never extra protons.
  3. “What changes if you change it?” Protons → the element. Neutrons → the mass (an isotope). Electrons → the charge (an ion).
  4. Something rubbed? Only electrons move. The two objects end up with equal and opposite charges.
  5. Draw a field? Electric: away from +, toward −. Gravity: always toward the mass. Magnetic: out of N, around, into S. Then a force arrow on the test particle.

The five patterns

1 · Labeling an atom

Cue: A drawing of a nucleus with dots around it, labeled “neutral.”

Method: Dots outside are electrons (−). Inside the nucleus, protons get + and neutrons get no sign. In a neutral atom, count the electrons: that's how many + you should have.

Worked example: Q1.

2 · Charging by rubbing

Cue: Balloon, sweater, hair, wool, “friction.”

Method: Before: both neutral (equal + and −). During: electrons move from one object to the other; + signs stay put. After: the object that gained electrons is −, the other is + by the same amount, and opposites attract.

Worked example: Q2. The Static electricity tab has an interactive version.

3 · Drawing a field

Cue: “Draw and label the field around …”

Worked example: Q3.

4 · The force on a particle in a field

Cue: “Draw a particle in the field and show the force.”

Method: A + test charge is pushed along the field arrows; a − test charge is pushed against them. A small mass is pulled toward the big mass. A small magnet's north end is pushed along the magnetic field line (away from N, toward S).

Worked example: Q4.

5 · Particle tables

Cue: A grid of protons, neutrons, electrons, mass, and charge with blanks.

Method: Use the three rules at the top of the checklist. Neutrons = mass − protons. Electrons = protons − charge (so a +1 ion has one fewer electron, a −2 ion has two more).

Worked examples: Q5 and Q6.

Q2Charging by rubbing

Show at the particle level what happens when a balloon is rubbed on a sweater (the balloon becomes negative): before, during, and when the balloon is held near the sweater afterward. Draw and label any fields and forces.

Solution

Cue: Balloon, sweater, friction.

Method: Only electrons move; charges end up equal and opposite; opposites attract.

Before. Both are neutral: draw equal numbers of + and − on each.

Rubbing. Electrons move from the sweater to the balloon. Draw a few − signs crossing over with arrows. The + signs don't move. The balloon now has extra − (negative), and the sweater has the same number of − missing (positive).

Held apart. Draw electric field lines from the + sweater to the − balloon (field points away from +, toward −). Draw two force arrows: one on the balloon pointing toward the sweater, one on the sweater pointing toward the balloon. They attract because opposite charges attract.

Electrons transfer sweater → balloon. Balloon −, sweater + (equal amounts). They attract; field lines go from sweater to balloon.

The explanation on the handed-in copy is right. Two small upgrades: say “electrons” (plural, since many move), and draw a force arrow on each object, not only one arrow between them. One arrow can read as the field instead of the forces.

Q3Drawing fields

Draw and label the appropriate fields around a positive charge, a large mass, and a bar magnet.

Solution

Cue: “Draw and label the field.”

Method: Field lines with arrows, not just a circle. The arrows carry the answer.

Positive charge → electric field. Straight lines pointing outward in every direction.

Large mass → gravitational field. Straight lines pointing inward toward the mass.

Bar magnet → magnetic field. Curved lines leaving the N end, looping around the magnet, and entering the S end.

The three diagrams in the Patterns tab are exactly what to draw.

Electric: arrows out from +. Gravitational: arrows into the mass. Magnetic: loops from N to S.

The handed-in copy names all three fields correctly but draws each as a circle. A circle doesn't show direction. Draw lines with arrowheads. The magnet especially needs lines that go from N, around, to S, rather than separate arrows at each end.

Q4Force in a field

Draw a particle in each field from Q3 and show and label the force that would act on it.

Solution

Cue: “Draw a particle … show the force.”

Method: Place a test particle, then draw one arrow from it in the direction it's pushed or pulled.

Electric field. A small − charge near the + charge: force arrow pointing toward the + (attract). Or a small + charge: arrow pointing away (repel). Either earns the point if it's labeled.

Gravitational field. A small mass: arrow pointing toward the large mass.

Magnetic field. A small magnet or compass needle: its north end is pushed along the field line, away from the bar's N and toward its S.

− charge pulled toward +; small mass pulled toward large mass; compass north end pushed from N toward S.

The electric part on the handed-in copy (a − pulled toward the +, a + pushed away) is right. The gravity and magnet parts don't clearly show a test particle with its own force arrow.

Strictly, an electric charge sitting still near a magnet feels no magnetic force. That's why the magnetic answer uses a small magnet. If the class used a different “particle” for this one, go with the teacher's version.

Every problem on this sheet uses one equation: the weighted average. What changes is which piece is missing. Spot the missing piece and the method follows.

The checklist

atomic mass = (mass₁ × fraction₁) + (mass₂ × fraction₂) + …fraction = percent ÷ 100, so 69.17% → 0.6917

Run through these when you first look at a problem.

  1. What's missing? All masses and percents given → find the atomic mass. The average and one mass given → find the other mass. Both masses and the average given → find the percents.
  2. Change percents to decimals (divide by 100). Or keep the percents and divide the whole sum by 100 at the end. Both give the same answer; just don't mix them.
  3. Where does the average come from? If the problem says “from the periodic table,” look it up and write it down before you start.
  4. Two unknown percents? Call one x and the other 100 − x, because they have to add to 100%.
  5. Sanity check. The atomic mass must land between the lightest and heaviest isotope, and closer to the most abundant one.

Don't round early in the “missing mass” problems. Dividing by the leftover fraction blows small rounding errors up. Keep every digit until the last step.

The four patterns

1 · Which isotope is most common? (no math)

Cue: Isotope names and the periodic-table mass, but no percents.

Method: The average is pulled toward the most abundant isotope. Whichever isotope's mass number is closest to the atomic mass is the most abundant.

Worked example: #1.

2 · Find the atomic mass (forward)

Cue: Every isotope's mass and percent are given. The question says “calculate the atomic mass.”

Method: Multiply each mass by its decimal abundance, then add. Three or four isotopes just means more terms.

(12.000 × 0.9945) + (14.003 × 0.0055) = 11.934 + 0.077 = 12.011 amu

Worked examples: #2, #3, #4, #6.

3 · Find a missing isotope mass (backward)

Cue: The question says “using the atomic mass on the periodic table, determine the mass of …”

Method: Write the forward equation with m where the missing mass goes. The missing percent is 100 minus the one you're given. Then undo the arithmetic: subtract the known term, divide by the leftover fraction.

average = (m₁ × f₁) + (m × f₂)m = (average − m₁ × f₁) ÷ f₂

Worked example: #5.

4 · Find the percents (two unknowns)

Cue: Both masses are given, the percents aren't, and the question says “calculate the abundance of each.”

Method: Let x be one percent and 100 − x the other, put them in the forward equation, distribute, and solve for x. This is the same linear-equation algebra as clearing a denominator.

[m₁·x + m₂(100 − x)] ÷ 100 = average

Worked example: #7.

A note on the worksheet's carbon example

The intro says carbon is mostly carbon-12 and carbon-14. Carbon's second stable isotope is actually carbon-13 (mass 13.003, about 1.1%). Carbon-14 is radioactive and only exists in trace amounts; it's the one used in carbon dating. The worksheet's arithmetic still works (it gives 12.011), so the method is fine to learn from. Just don't memorize “carbon-14 is the second most common isotope,” because it isn't.
